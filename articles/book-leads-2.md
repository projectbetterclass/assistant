**The argument.** A lead is just a person you can contact. What you want are engaged leads: people who show interest in what you sell. The way to get them is to offer something they want, either your core offer or a lead magnet, a free or cheap complete solution to a narrow problem that reveals the next problem, which your paid offer solves.

## Leads alone aren't enough
- **A lead.** Anyone you can contact: the numbers in your phone, an email list, people on the street.
- **An engaged lead.** Someone who shows interest: leaves their details, follows you and can be contacted, replies to your email. Engaged leads are the true output of advertising.
- **The webinar and the case study.** Hormozi spent eight Sundays building a webinar: $450 of ads, 80 leads, no sales, nobody watched it. A 13-minute screen recording of one gym's real ad results, with "book a call" at the end, filled the calendar overnight. They didn't want the webinar; they did want the case study.
- **Core offer first.** Advertise what you sell; that may be all you need. If people want to know more before they buy (often for pricier things), lead with a lead magnet.

## What a lead magnet is
- **A complete solution to a narrow problem.** Solving it reveals the next problem, which your core offer solves. Salty pretzels at a bar solve hunger and make you thirsty; the drinks pay for the pretzels.
- **Three types.** Reveal their problem (a diagnosis: a site speed test, a posture check, a termite inspection); a sample or trial of the real thing; or one step of a multi-step process. A shop's fitting room is all three at once.
- **Four ways to deliver.** Software (a calculator, a dashboard), information (a mini course), a service (a free audit, a first coat of sealant) or a physical product. Three types times four ways gives up to twelve lead magnets for one problem.

## Seven steps to a lead magnet
1. **The problem, and who for.** Narrow and meaningful, chosen so your core offer solves the problem it reveals.
2. **How to solve it.** Pick one of the three types.
3. **How to deliver it.** Pick one of the four ways, or make several and rotate them.
4. **Test what to name it.** Headline first, then image, then subheadline. The book's own title won test after test ("Leads" beat "Advertising" 58 to 42); dropping "How to" from the subtitle lost 70 to 30.
5. **Easy to consume.** Every format: phone and computer, video, text and audio. Hormozi's first book splits about evenly between e-book, print, audio and video.
6. **Make it darn good.** **Give away the secrets, sell the implementation.** Free fluff makes people think you're fluff; value before they buy makes them expect value after.
7. **Easy to say they want more.** A call to action: what to do, in clear words ("call now"), and a reason to do it now: real **scarcity** (only five spots a week), **urgency** (a bonus that ends Friday), or any reason at all, since even a made-up one beats none.

## What it's worth
- Without a lead magnet: $1,000 of ads per sales call, one in three closes, so $3,000 a customer.
- With a $25 lead magnet: $75 of ads per call, $100 all in, one in ten buys, so $1,000 a customer. Three times the customers for the same budget.

**The idea worth keeping.** Give people something they want before you ask them to buy: a lead magnet so good they'd pay for it, which solves one narrow problem and makes the next one obvious.

**Try it on your work.** Pick one narrow problem your AI service's buyers have before they'd ever pay you. Design the lead magnet: its type, how it's delivered, a headline, and the call to action with a reason to act now.
