**The claim.** Humanoid robots may become the biggest product category ever, because one machine could do useful work for almost every business and family. Figure's new demo is a real step toward a robot you can put to work without rebuilding your home around it.

## What Figure showed
- One model, Helix 2.5, tidied a living room, made a bed and folded towels in a house it had never been in, with objects it had never seen.
- Figure says it tested 30 Bay Area homes with no training data from any of them, and it "succeeded in all of them".
- The training data comes from "Index": over 90,000 people a week record themselves doing everyday things, 35 minutes of footage every second. A model trained without it "just didn't work".
- Figure claims a scaling law like the one for chatbots: the robot gets steadily better each time that data doubles. It is committing $3.5B to computing power for it.
- The catch is on screen, not in the pitch: in Figure's own evaluation, **56% of tasks were finished in full.** "Succeeded in every home" means it did some of the work in every home.

## Why it could matter
- **Care:** an elderly parent keeps living at home while a robot changes the bedding and carries the shopping. The WHO expects one in six people to be 60 or older by 2030.
- **Hospitals:** fetching equipment and restocking cupboards so staff spend more time on patients.
- **Danger:** contaminated material, damaged buildings, rescues. Stairs, handles and tools are built for the human shape, which is the case for a humanoid.

In Morgan Stanley's forecast, about 90% of the robots work in industry and business, not homes.

**Risks he names.** A 56% success rate, plus safety, reliability and upkeep cost decide what becomes a product. Workers who lose jobs don't share the gains automatically.

**The stock.** None. Figure is private, and so is Agility Robotics, whose Digit 5 robot is shown. There is nothing to buy from this video.

**Aswath check.** Two forecasts for the same market differ by more than 100 times. That isn't a number, it's a story, and Aswath's rule is story first, then check it's possible, plausible and probable (lesson L02). A big market also doesn't tell you who wins it. When every robot company is priced to win, the total is more than the market.
