**The idea.** When demand goes up, cut supply and the price goes up. A charity poker night raised its ticket from $10,000 to $25,000 and sold more. A service can use the same lever: fewer spots, a waiting list, and a price that matches what your keenest buyers would pay.

## The examples
- The charity night: $10,000 to $25,000 a seat, and more seats sold. The advice came from a high-end jeweller: "When demand increases, you cut supply."
- Chanel stores get one or two of each bag, with no warning of what's coming. Every bag feels like the last one.
- A day of his time for five people at $20,000 each leaves money behind if one or two would have paid $200,000. Three people at $100,000 earns $300,000 instead of $100,000, and three are easier to look after than five.

## What to do with it
- **Marketing moves the demand curve.** Textbooks treat demand as fixed. Telling more people moves the whole curve, so the same supply can carry a higher price.
- **"Full" is your call.** You decide your capacity. Cap it at, say, five clients and offer a waiting list. A waiting list makes people think you must be good.
- **Let the steam out slowly.** Open a few spots at a time. If you're always available, the pressure, which is your pricing power, never builds.
- If you've ever felt relief hearing a price ("I'd have paid way more"), the seller left money on the table.

**Watch for.** Scarcity only works when it's real. Saying you're full when you aren't is a lie customers can catch. With a new service you really are limited by your hours, so use that honestly.

**Try it this week.** Work out your real capacity for a side service: hours a week divided by hours per client. Write the number down and price for it, not for "anyone who'll pay". Ties to P08.
