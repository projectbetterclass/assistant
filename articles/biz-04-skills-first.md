**The idea.** When you're new, the skills that matter most are selling, building the offer and communicating. Finance, negotiation and deal-making can wait. Learn to sell by doing it yourself: the calls are where you learn who your customer is.

## Her ranking, for a new business
| Tier | Skill | Why |
|---|---|---|
| S | Sales | The founder is the first and best salesperson. Calls show you who it's for, and who it isn't. |
| S | Offer | What you give, what it costs, how they pay. Get it right and everything else is easier. |
| S | Communication | She moved it up mid-video: listening and seeing it from their side is how you find the offer. |
| A | Product | Solve one problem very well, and expect to change it 5–10 times. Word of mouth is the test. |
| A | Marketing | Pick one channel where your customers already are, and get it working well. |
| B | Managing people | Onboard well: give context, then the skills the job needs. |
| B | Hiring | Recruiting is marketing, aimed at employees. |
| C | Finance | Learn to read a profit-and-loss statement; pay a firm to make it. |
| D | Selling your company | Irrelevant until you have something people buy. |
| Last | Negotiation | Later. Start by asking what the other side wants. |

## Two examples she gives
- **Selling without "selling":** she was told she was just signing people up for a gym. Be honest about what you offer and who it's for.
- **An offer that changed everything:** Gym Launch went from $10,000 up front for 16 weeks to $16,000, paid $1,000 a week out of the money the programme was making the gym. Higher price, lower risk for the customer, better results.

Once a business is bigger, every skill moves to the top tier, because you need a team that's good at all of them.

**Try it this week.** Count the times you've talked someone into something: a job, tutoring, a club. If it's under ten, selling is your first skill to practise. The next two reads are how. Ties to P04 (ask about their life, not your idea).
