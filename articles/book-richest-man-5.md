**The argument.** Money you have attracts people who want some of it, and misfortunes nobody can predict. Rodan learns from the gold lender Mathon how to judge a loan and keep the principal safe; the old soldier Banzar watches Babylon's walls hold off an army. Both say the same thing: protect what you have before you need to.

## The gold lender
- **Rodan's problem.** Rodan, a spear maker, has been given fifty pieces of gold by the king. Rodan's sister wants it lent to Aram, the sister's husband, to set up as a rich merchant, and saying no to family is hard. Rodan goes to Mathon, not to borrow but for advice, which surprises Mathon: nobody asks a gold lender for advice.
- **The token chest.** Mathon keeps a token from every borrower until the loan is repaid, and tells the stories behind them. The safest loans are to people whose possessions are worth more than the loan. Next come people with steady earnings from work, like the farmer helped through a plague of locusts who repaid when the next crop came in. Riskiest are loans to people with neither, unless good friends vouch for them.
- **The ones never repaid.** Loans to people in the grip of strong emotions, and to people who wanted to start a trade they didn't know.
- **The advice.** First, keep it safe: Aram isn't wise in guarding gold, so help family in other ways. Second, let it earn, and be conservative about how much: plans promising huge returns come from dreamers. Lend only to people and businesses with an established record.
- **The motto.** Carved inside the chest's lid: "Better a little caution than a great regret."

## The walls of Babylon
- **The siege.** While Babylon's army is away fighting the Elamites, the Assyrians attack from the north. Old Banzar guards the passage up to the walls, and reassures the frightened crowd, a child among them: the walls will hold. After days of fighting, they do.
- **The lesson.** Babylon lasted because it was well protected. Our walls today are insurance, savings and dependable investments: we can't afford to be without them.

**The idea worth keeping.** Protect the principal: lend only where repayment is secure, help people in ways that don't risk your savings, and build your walls before the attack comes.

**Try it on your work.** Check your FIRE plan's emergency buffer: how many months of fixed costs would it cover? If it's less than you'd want, add a monthly step toward it.
