**The claim.** The Treasury bought back 30-year bonds for 52 cents on the dollar. That isn't a fire sale, it's basic bond math. Every usual way to bring long-term rates down is blocked right now, so figure out whether you're paying or collecting before the next move.

## The math, in one example
- A bond sold in Feb 2021 pays $1.88 a year per $100. A new one pays $5.30. To sell the old one today, it has to be cheap enough that a buyer doesn't mind the low coupon: $52.
- Sell at $52 and reinvest at 5.3%, and you have about $190 by 2051. Hold to the end and you have about $193. Only someone forced to sell early really loses.
- It works the same as a mortgage: a 2.7% loan on a $500K house is ~$2,000 a month, and at 6.5% it's ~$3,200.
- The biggest holder of those cheap bonds is the Fed, with ~$472B of paper losses that it never has to realize.

## Why rates aren't coming down
| Lever | 2023 | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Treasury borrows short instead | Used | Locked through end of 2026 |
| Fed cuts | Used | Talking about hikes |
| Fed buys bonds | – | Stopped in August |
| Congress spends less | – | "Never going to happen" |

- The 30-year yield went from 1.56% to 5.31%, the highest since 2007. Britain's is the highest since 1998, Germany's since 2011, Japan's 10-year since 1996.
- Japan, the biggest foreign lender at $1.12T, has cut ~$120B since February.
- Even Google pays more: it borrowed for 40 years at 5.75% in February and 6.5% in August. Half of that is higher Treasury rates and half is lenders worried about $200B a year of data-center spending.
- You may own bonds without knowing it: a 2030 target-date fund is ~40% bonds. Bond funds are down ~19% from the 2020 peak, but only a few percent counting the interest.

## His four scenarios
1. **Jobs crack:** long rates fall, and bond funds and home buyers win. Cash loses.
2. **A bad 30-year auction:** rates rise, borrowers and their shareholders pay, and lenders win.
3. **The Fed hikes anyway:** short rates rise, but long rates can fall if the market expects something to break, as in 2000, 2006 and 2018. Credit cards lose and cash wins.
4. **Japan's money goes home:** US rates rise and the dollar falls. Gold holders win.

**How the call aged.** Scenario 3 happened: the Fed hiked on Sep 16. Long rates didn't fall, though. The 30-year rose from 5.29% to 5.60% and the 10-year from 4.84% to 5.24%, so a long-bond fund (TLT) is down 4.6%. Cash finally got a raise.

**Aswath check.** This is your riskfree rate (L03). At a 5.2% 10-year, every valuation starts from a higher base than a year ago. Brian's Google example is your cost of debt (L06): the riskfree rate plus a spread that widens when lenders doubt the spending.
