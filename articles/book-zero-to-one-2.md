**The argument.** A business can create a lot of value and still make no money. What matters is how much of the value it keeps. In perfect competition, prices fall until nobody profits. A monopoly, a company so good at its niche that nothing close substitutes for it, keeps the profit and can plan for the long run.

## Chapter 3: all happy companies are different
- US airlines carry millions of people, yet in his example they made about 37 cents a passenger. Google kept roughly a fifth of its revenue as profit.
- **Everyone lies about it.** Monopolies play down their dominance ("we're one small player in a huge market"). Companies in brutal competition exaggerate how unique they are by defining their market as a tiny intersection: "the only British food restaurant in Palo Alto".
- Profits let a monopoly care about its people and its products; a competitive business can only think about this month.

## Chapter 4: the ideology of competition
- We're trained to compete: grades, rankings, rivals. Competition feels like proof of value, but it makes people copy each other and lose sight of what matters.
- Rivals obsess over each other and both lose. PayPal and Elon Musk's X.com fought hard in 1999 and merged in 2000, which saved them both.
- "If you can't beat a rival, it may be better to merge."

**The idea worth keeping.** Be honest about your market. If you have to squeeze it into a tiny intersection to sound unique, you're in a crowded market wearing a costume.

**Try it on your work.** Describe your idea's market in one plain sentence. Then list three things a customer could use instead. If the list is long, find a smaller group you could serve better than anyone. This feeds P03 (own a tiny market first).
