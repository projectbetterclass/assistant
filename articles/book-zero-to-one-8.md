**The argument.** The last chapters pull the book together. Computers will work best alongside people rather than replacing them. The cleantech bubble shows what happens when businesses can't answer the basic questions. And founders tend to be unusual people, which is both their strength and their risk.

## Chapter 12: man and machine
- People and computers are good at different things. The big opportunities are in tools that help people do more (PayPal's fraud system flagged suspicious payments and let humans decide), not in replacing them.

## Chapter 13: seeing green, the seven questions
Most cleantech companies of the 2000s failed because they couldn't answer these:
1. **Engineering:** is it a breakthrough, not a small improvement?
2. **Timing:** is now the right time to start?
3. **Monopoly:** are you starting with a big share of a small market?
4. **People:** do you have the right team?
5. **Distribution:** can you actually get it to customers?
6. **Durability:** will you still be ahead in 10 and 20 years?
7. **Secret:** have you seen an opportunity others don't?

## Chapter 14 and the end
- Founders are often extreme in contradictory ways: insider and outsider, admired and attacked. The danger is believing your own myth.
- The future isn't guaranteed to be better. Whether it is depends on people building new things.

**The idea worth keeping.** You won't get seven yeses. Thiel says to get as many as you can, and to be honest about the ones you can't.

**Try it on your work.** These are the seven questions on your idea board. Open the starred idea in Building products and answer each with yes, not sure or no. A "not sure" is a question to go and find out. This is P12.
