**The argument.** Every offer solves one problem and reveals the next; an upsell is whatever you offer next to solve it. Often the upsells make most of the profit: your first offer doesn't have to make money if the second, third and fourth do. Upsells fail when you offer something they don't want, at the wrong time, or in a way they don't believe.

## Why upsells make the money
- **The burger shop.** 25 cents of profit on a $2 burger. Fries add 75 cents, making it a meal brings it to $2 (eight times), supersizing to $3 (twelve times). Without fries and soda there'd be no McDonald's.
- **More, better, or new.** Upsells offer more of the same (quantity), a better version (quality), or something new that goes with it.

## The classic upsell: you can't have X without Y
- **The story.** A fur coat dealer advertised free earmuffs with coat storage, then: "and we'll store those as well for $30. You don't want to store anything else, do you?" People say no to the second question and yes to the first.
- **How it works.** Offer the solution to the next problem the moment they notice it: a sealant after the car wash; a helmet, lights and puncture-proof tyres with the bike; a nutrition course with the exercise course.
- **Actually do it.** Businesses that sell one thing barely have a business, just a front end; adding one upsell has 5x'd some.
- **Tactics.** Offer the most profitable upsell first. Save bonuses to add one at a time, and give them all anyway if they say yes early. Sell more in a hyper-buying cycle (weddings, new hobbies, babies, moves). Get it into their hands as soon as you can. Name bundles ("fastest results bundle"). Build the upsell into how you deliver ("book a meeting from a meeting"). Charge 5 to 50% more for guarantees, warranties and insurance: an art studio charging 10% for replacement cover sold it to 30% of customers.
- **The magnetic middle.** Small $5, medium $8, large $9 moves medium buyers to large; small $6, medium $7 moves small buyers to medium.

## The menu upsell: you don't need that, you need this
- **The story.** After 19 nutrition consults with no supplement sales, Hormozi forgot the script and just asked "chocolate or vanilla?", then "want to use the card on file?" The next 20 bought. Writing personal instructions on the order form, and crossing out what someone didn't need, sold even more.
- **Unsell.** Tell them what they don't need ("you're not trying to gain weight, so cross that out"). It builds the trust to sell what they do.
- **Prescribe.** Tell them exactly what they need and how to use it, as if they already have it.
- **A or B.** Ask which they prefer, not whether they want it: one bottle or two, morning or afternoon, call or text. Add a nudge: "this is my favourite".
- **Card on file.** "Want to use the card on file?" removes the hassle of paying.
- **The Economist play.** Digital $59, print $125, digital plus print $125: people take both.

## The anchor upsell: show the premium first
- **The story.** With a $500 budget for a suit, Hormozi tried one on and saw $16,000 on the tag. "Do you care about the designer?" The next one was $2,200, plus $300 of extras, and it felt cheap.
- **The steps.** Present the anchor (five to ten times your main offer), get the gasp, come to the rescue ("do you care what makes it premium?"), present the main offer with the same core function, ask how they'd like to pay.
- **Make it real.** A fake anchor doesn't work: offer a premium you'd happily deliver, and some will buy it. Worse than a $1,000 offer to someone with $100 is a $100 offer to someone with $1,000.

## The rollover upsell: credit what they paid toward the next thing
- **The story.** Hormozi's money-back winners used their $600 on three free months, then left before paying anything. A friend's gym rolled the winnings into a year instead: $50 off every month for 12 months, so they paid from day one.
- **Who.** Old customers (win-backs), your own upset customers (before a refund), competitors' upset customers (credit what they still owe elsewhere), and current customers.
- **How.** Up front or spread over time, priced so the next offer is at least four times the credit (a discount of 25% at most), as a one-time offer.
- **Win-backs.** Personal videos to 200 past customers offering $4,000 of credit: about 20% came back, $1.9 million a year from one day of recording.
- **The gift card play.** $200 gift cards for $20, two per customer, only for friends; roll them into an offer of at least $1,000.

**The idea worth keeping.** The money is in what you offer next: solve the problem your last offer just revealed, at the moment it appears, in a way that's easy to say yes to.

**Try it on your work.** For your AI service, write the next three problems a client hits after the first project, and the upsell for each. Then write the A-or-B question you'd ask for the first one.
