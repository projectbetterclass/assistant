**The argument.** The questions that matter most are often the ones you're afraid to ask, because the answer might kill your idea. Ask them anyway, early. And don't turn learning into formal meetings: a casual chat gets more honest answers than an "interview".

## Chapter 3: asking important questions
- **Find the scary questions.** The ones that could prove your idea wrong ("would anyone actually pay for this?") are the ones worth asking.
- **Prepare your three big questions** before every conversation. What do you most need to learn right now? They change as you learn.
- **Look before you zoom.** Don't jump straight into the detail of one problem. First check that it's a problem they actually care about.
- **Love bad news.** A clear "no" is progress. A vague "maybe" is the real waste.

## Chapter 4: keeping it casual
- If it's a formal "interview", people get stiff and polite. If it's a chat about their work or life, they tell you what's really going on.
- You don't need a meeting. Many good conversations take five minutes, at an event, in a queue, over coffee.
- Strip out the formality: no "I'm doing some research", no survey voice.

**The idea worth keeping.** Write your three questions down before you talk to anyone. Without them, you'll drift into pleasant chat and learn nothing.

**Try it on your work.** Write the scariest question about your idea. Then write how you could ask it casually, as part of a normal conversation about their week. This is P04.
