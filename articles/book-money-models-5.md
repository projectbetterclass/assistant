**The argument.** When someone says no, it's no to this offer, not to every offer. A downsell finds the highest-value version for their budget, in one of two ways: change how they pay, or change what they get. What you never do is offer the same thing for less, because that trades trust for a sale.

## How not to downsell
- **The car insurance.** A salesman kept cutting the price of the same insurance from $5,000 down to $400. The buyer said no at the start because of the price, and no at the end because of the salesman, and started doubting the price of the car too.
- **The rules.** No means no to this thing. A downsell is a trade: if you give something, get something. Personalize, don't pressure: offer more of what they like and less of what they don't. Have 100 ways to offer what you already have, not 100 products. Never drop the price just to close; customers talk.

## Payment plan downsells: how much can you put down today?
- **The story.** In Hormozi's first month, a lead couldn't afford half now or a third now, but could pay in full on the first. Card taken, charged on the second: the first payment plan.
- **Why it works.** "It costs too much" usually means "it costs too much up front". Payment plans get more buyers and still collect full price, as long as they don't move people who would have paid in full.
- **The seven steps, stopping when they buy.**
  1. Reward paying in full rather than punishing paying over time: show the price with interest included, then the discount for prepaying.
  2. Third-party financing, a credit card ("would you rather I decide the terms, or you?"), or layaway.
  3. Half now, half on their next payday, or "what's the most you can put down today?"
  4. Check they still want it: 1 to 10. Eight or above, keep going; seven or below, ask why not a 10, and offer something different.
  5. A third now, a third on each of the next two paydays.
  6. Even payments across the whole service (16 weekly payments for a 16-week programme).
  7. A free trial, with conditions.
- **Seesaw selling.** "Would you rather have giant monthly payments or tiny ones?" Then: the more they put down, the lower the monthly rate.
- **The tricks.** Keep offering the pay-in-full discount during the plan (customers forget it). Charge on paydays, and retry a declined card a few times that day: a third of declines get recovered. Bill less often: churn was 10.7% a month with monthly billing, 5% quarterly and 2% yearly, across 14,000 businesses.

## Trial with penalty: start free, if you do the work
- **The story.** An HR software company offered free onboarding if the customer did their training, and charged for it if not. The training was done, the software got used, and they never switched.
- **How it works.** The mirror of "win your money back": they pay only if they don't do what makes customers succeed. Use it as a downsell: if your main offer closes 3 in 10, a trial might take 4 more, and 3 of those stay. Six instead of three.
- **The five steps.** Offer it last ("how about we just get you started for free?"). Always take a card: no card, no trial. Sell staying and paying ("if this gets you the result, will you stay long term?"). Explain the fees after the card, and have them initial the fee clause. Make the check-ins required: they're the upsell meetings.
- **After the trial.** Liked it: offer longer or more. Hated it: ask what should have been different, take the blame, offer the better fit (about half buy). Didn't use it: reach out before the deadline and waive the fee if they meet you.
- **Pay less now or later vs. trial with penalty.** Use the first for products and one-time services, this one for recurring services where results depend on the customer's work. A $1 trial gets a working card on file.

## Feature downsells: change what they get
- **The story.** A friend cut the money-back guarantee to lower the price: "if you don't want the option to get your money back, you can pay less." Close rates went from 25% to 75%, and more people bought the full-price option, because they only saw the guarantee's value once it was gone.
- **How it works.** Take something away, lower the price, ask "how about now?" Less quantity (one month instead of three), lower quality (vinyl instead of leather, overnight replies instead of five-minute ones), or a whole feature removed (drop the calls, keep chat and email).
- **Remove from highest value to lowest.** The first cut makes people reconsider the original offer; the later cuts find their best deal.
- **Done for you, to do it yourself.** A chiropractor's home tools; a painter leasing out a spray machine.
- **Tactics.** Never negotiate price ("business terrorists"). Name the packages, with the cheapest one called "the minimum". After two cuts, ask how badly they want it on a scale of 1 to 10. Ask "deal?" or "fair enough?" after each step. Offer a free orientation to sell the do-it-yourself version. Downsell current customers to only what they use before they cancel. Trade a discount for reviews, testimonials and introductions.

**The idea worth keeping.** A "no" is a no to this version. Change when they pay or what they get, never the price of the same thing, and you keep both the sale and their trust.

**Try it on your work.** Write the downsell ladder for your AI service: the full offer, a payment plan, a trial with penalty, and two feature cuts (what you'd remove first and second).
