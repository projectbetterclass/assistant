**The argument.** You can expect the future to be better or worse, and you can treat it as something you plan (definite) or something that just happens (indefinite). Thiel thinks a culture of indefinite optimism, keeping options open and hoping, leads to diversified mediocrity. Success isn't luck you wait for; it comes from a plan you commit to.

## Chapter 6: four views of the future
| | Definite | Indefinite |
|---|---|---|
| Optimistic | Plan and build a better future | Things will improve, but nobody plans how |
| Pessimistic | The future is bleak, so prepare | Things will get worse, and we don't know what to do |

- The "well-rounded" student who does a bit of everything is the indefinite type: lots of options, no conviction.
- Treating your life or business as a lottery ticket means never committing to a plan good enough to be wrong about.

## Chapter 7: follow the money
- **The power law.** A few outcomes dominate everything else. In a good venture fund, the single best investment often returns more than all the others combined.
- So spread-everywhere thinking fails: you should only invest in, or work on, things that could be that one big winner.
- The same applies to you: be excellent at one thing rather than average at many.

**The idea worth keeping.** Keeping your options open feels safe but stops you from going deep enough for anything to work. Pick one thing and commit long enough to find out.

**Try it on your work.** Look at your goals and projects. Which one could matter more than all the others combined? Give it the best hours of your week, and say no to one thing to make room.
