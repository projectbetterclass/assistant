**The argument.** The first law. A habit starts with a cue, so make the cues for good habits impossible to miss and the cues for bad ones hard to see.

## Chapter 4: the man who didn't look right
- With enough practice the brain spots patterns without thinking. That's why habits slip out of awareness, the bad ones included.
- Japanese train operators point at each signal and call it out loud. This "pointing and calling" cuts mistakes by up to 85%.
- **The Habits Scorecard:** list what you do on a normal day, from waking up on, and mark each habit good (+), bad (−) or neutral (=). Don't judge; just notice.

## Chapter 5: the best way to start a new habit
- **Implementation intention:** "I will [habit] at [time] in [place]." In one exercise study, 91% of people who wrote this down exercised at least weekly, against about 35–38% of those who didn't.
- **Habit stacking:** "After [a habit I already have], I will [the new habit]." Your existing routine becomes the cue.

## Chapter 6: motivation is overrated; environment often matters more
- In a hospital cafeteria, putting bottled water by the tills and in more fridges raised water sales by about a quarter and cut soda sales by about a tenth. Nobody was persuaded; the room changed.
- Put the cue for a good habit where you'll see it: the book on your pillow, the guitar in the middle of the room.
- One space, one use: if the bed is for sleep, the desk for work, each place cues one thing.

## Chapter 7: the secret to self-control
- Many US soldiers used heroin in Vietnam, yet only about 5% relapsed within a year of coming home. Their surroundings changed, and so did the cues.
- People with "great self-control" mostly just spend less time near temptation. Remove the cue: the phone in another room beats willpower.

**Try it this week.** Give one piece in the app a "+ when" in this exact form: "after [existing habit], at [time], in [place]". For example: "After breakfast, 7:30, at my desk: 20 minutes of Atomic Habits."
