**The argument.** The second law. We act because we expect a reward, so make the habits you need look like something you want, and surround yourself with people for whom they're normal.

## Chapter 8: how to make a habit irresistible
- Gulls will peck at an exaggerated fake beak over a real one, and junk food works the same way: an exaggerated version of what we're wired to want.
- Dopamine rises when we *expect* a reward, not only when we get it. Wanting drives action more than liking does.
- **Temptation bundling:** pair something you need to do with something you want to do. One student rigged his exercise bike so Netflix only played while he pedalled.
- Stack it: "After [current habit], I'll do [habit I need]. After [habit I need], I'll do [habit I want]."

## Chapter 9: the role of family and friends in shaping your habits
- László Polgár set out to show that genius is trained, and raised three daughters who became chess champions.
- We copy three groups: **the close** (family, friends), **the many** (the crowd), and **the powerful** (people with status).
- Join a group where your habit is the normal thing to do. Belonging beats willpower.

## Chapter 10: how to find and fix the causes of your bad habits
- A craving is a surface version of a deeper need: connection, status, less uncertainty, rest.
- Feelings come from the story you tell about a habit. Change "I *have* to study" to "I *get* to study"; read nerves before a talk as excitement.
- A motivation ritual: do something you enjoy right before a hard habit, and over time the two become linked.

**Compare it with your Buddha goal.** Clear treats craving as fuel, something to steer toward better habits. Buddhist teaching treats craving as the root of suffering, something to see clearly and let go of. Where do they agree, and where not? It's a good question for your map.

**Try it this week.** Bundle one need with one want: Atomic Habits only on your walk, or your favourite drink only while you do the FIRE cost breakdown.
