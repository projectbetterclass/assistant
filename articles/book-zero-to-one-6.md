**The argument.** Thiel's law: a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed. The first decisions, who you start with, who owns what, how people are paid, are the hardest to undo. Get them right, then build a team so aligned it looks like a tight-knit "mafia" (the PayPal founders were later nicknamed the PayPal Mafia).

## Chapter 9: foundations
- **Choose a co-founder like a marriage partner.** Skills matter, but how well you know each other and work together matters more.
- **Ownership, possession and control** (who owns the equity, who runs it day to day, who formally governs it) cause trouble when they drift apart.
- **Small boards work.** Three people is ideal; big boards can't hold anyone to account.
- **Low cash, more ownership.** A CEO paid a modest salary stays focused on the company's long-term value. Everyone should be paid in a way that rewards building value later, not taking it now.

## Chapter 10: the mechanics of mafia
- Hire people who'll enjoy working together and stay, not just people with the best CVs.
- To recruit, explain why your mission is important and why your team is special. Perks don't attract the right people.
- **Give each person one thing to own.** At PayPal, giving everyone exactly one job and judging them on it removed most internal fights.
- A strong culture is close to a cult in its conviction, but without the cult's isolation.

**The idea worth keeping.** Even a one-person service has a foundation: who you work with, how you split money, what you promise. Decide those carefully at the start.

**Try it on your work.** If you'd start something with someone, write down now who would own what and who decides when you disagree. If that's awkward to write, it'll be worse to fix later.
