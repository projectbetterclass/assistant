**The argument.** Habits work best in areas that suit you. The real enemy is boredom, not failure, and even good habits need regular review so they don't harden into autopilot.

## Chapter 18: the truth about talent
- Michael Phelps and runner Hicham El Guerrouj differ in height by several inches but have legs of about the same length. Each body suits its sport.
- Genes don't decide your destiny, but they decide where you have an edge. Choose habits that fit your nature.
- To find where you have an edge, ask: what feels like fun to me but work to others? What makes me lose track of time? Where do I get better returns than most?
- Explore first, then focus. If you can't be the best at one thing, combine two skills few people have together.

## Chapter 19: the Goldilocks rule
- Steve Martin spent about 18 years on comedy: roughly ten learning, four refining, four as a star.
- **The Goldilocks rule:** motivation peaks on tasks just past your current ability, not too easy and not too hard.
- The bigger threat is boredom. Professionals stick to the schedule; amateurs wait until they feel like it. Learn to love doing the same thing again.

## Chapter 20: the downside of creating good habits
- Habits free your attention, but on autopilot you stop noticing mistakes. Mastery is habits plus deliberate practice.
- The Lakers under Pat Riley tracked every player's numbers and aimed for small yearly gains on their career best.
- **Reflect and review:** Clear writes a yearly review (what went well, what didn't, what I learned) and checks his life against his values.
- Hold your identity loosely. "I'm an athlete" can break when your sport ends; "I'm someone who's mentally tough" can last.

## Conclusion: the secret to results that last
One coin doesn't make you rich, and one push-up doesn't make you strong, but enough of them do. Keep looking for the next 1%.
